Case study 01

Establishing design in a service with 3.8 million members

OrganisationNHS Business Services Authority
ServiceNHS Pensions
RoleSenior Service Designer
PeriodJun 2024 — Feb 2026

Situation

NHS Pensions serves 3.8 million members. When I joined there was no design team, and no agile delivery practice in the directorate either.

I was part of the group that set up the DDaT function in Pensions. My remit was to establish the user-centred design team and build the service design practice from scratch. At that point there was one proof-of-concept squad.

Approach

When you start from zero, the obvious move is to prove design's value quickly by producing good work. I decided the more useful thing was to build a practice that would keep working after the first designers moved on.

That meant putting structure, documentation and shared method ahead of output, and accepting slower visible progress in the first year. It also meant the function could take on designers at any level and split across four workstreams without losing consistency.

What I did

  • Built the delivery practice alongside the design practice. Agile ways of working arrived in the directorate with DDaT. The proof-of-concept squad became four multidisciplinary teams, each owning a different part of the member journey. The design function had to be built to fit that structure from the start.
  • Coached five service designers, at levels from apprentice to mid-weight. Regular 1:1s and design critique sessions. I supported them on running workshops, presenting to stakeholders and developing their practice generally. One has since progressed to senior.
  • Made design decisions traceable. I ran sessions on problem definition and prioritisation, and set up a standardised design log recording how and why each decision was made. The team also contributed to the Pensions-wide RAID log.
  • Built a shared library of templates and documentation. Blueprints, journey maps and workshop formats designers could adapt instead of starting from scratch each time. We held accessibility to WCAG 2.2 AA, backed by audits, and ran heuristic evaluations on selected products to find the larger structural issues.

Outcome

The team now starts with the assumptions and the problem, instead of pushing for a technical solution before understanding the landscape.

That holds without me prompting it. Design decisions are recorded in a form other disciplines can read, and the team has monthly show and tells and far more contact with operational colleagues and senior stakeholders than it did before.

The lead delivery manager had never worked with a design team and started out wanting to deliver fast, even where that cost quality. Over the two years they became the person making the case internally for doing discovery and defining the problem first.

One product outcome from the period: a digital calculation tool that automated a manual process and cut user task completion time by 50%.

Case study 02

Establishing design practice across the organisation

OrganisationNHS Business Services Authority
ScopeFour service areas · DDaT-wide
RoleSenior Service Designer · GDS assessor
Period2023 — present

Situation

Design practice built inside one team tends to stay there.

The templates work and the documentation standard holds, but none of it travels. Nobody outside has a reason to look, and there is no way in for someone joining a different directorate.

The people who decide whether design gets to do its job are often not designers. They are the delivery managers, policy leads, operational managers and service owners who choose when design is involved and what it is allowed to change.

Approach

Define what good design practice looks like here, and then hold it there. That means writing the standard down so people can find it and follow it, making the case for design to colleagues outside digital, and measuring quality against the GDS Service Standard rather than local opinion.

What I did

  • Wrote the service design section of the organisation's DDaT playbook. It sets out our practices and processes for people who don't already know them: new joiners, colleagues in other disciplines, and contractors who need to get productive quickly. The playbook belongs to the organisation rather than to my team, so it works as a shared reference.
  • Set the documentation and template standard, and got it adopted across four service areas. It started as one team's working library of blueprints, journey maps, workshop formats and a design log. Four separate services now use it, so consistency doesn't depend on which team you happen to be in.
  • Made the case for design to people who don't work in digital. Counter-fraud, policy, operations and communications. Delivery managers who had never worked with a design team. Service owners deciding where to spend. What convinces them is seeing that time spent defining the problem gives a better result than building the first idea, so we ran monthly show and tells until that became the expectation.
  • Worked with the GDS Service Standard from both sides. I'm a trained service assessor and have sat on panels several times, as well as being responsible for teams delivering through discovery, alpha and beta. Assessing other teams shows me which parts of the standard are commonly misread, and that shapes the guidance I write and how I prepare my own teams.

Outcome

A designer joining a different service area now finds documented method, templates they can adapt and a written account of how service design works here. None of that existed before. Colleagues outside design have a reference for what to expect from designers and when to involve them.

Quality is measured against the GDS Service Standard rather than against whoever is most senior in the room.

About

I'm a senior service designer working on UK public services. I'm currently at the NHS Business Services Authority, where I set up the user-centred design function in NHS Pensions and led the design response to a counter-fraud problem on a national support scheme.

I'm a multidisciplinary designer and I've worked hands-on across all of user-centred design: service design, content design, interaction design, user research and design systems. That breadth is deliberate. In complex public services, problems rarely arrive with the right label on them. Something that looks like a content problem is often an interaction problem, and something that looks like an interaction problem is usually a service problem further upstream. Working across all of those disciplines means I can diagnose the problem before choosing a tool, and lead teams made up of people who do all of them.

I work across the full service design lifecycle, from understanding a complex problem through to implementation, front-stage and back-stage. That includes journey maps and blueprints that find where a service fails people, systems thinking for services that cross organisational boundaries, and design decisions that take account of people, process, policy and technology rather than the interface alone.

In regulated services, most of what users experience as bad design is a policy decision, an operational constraint or a system boundary showing through. I'd rather change the decision than style the symptom. That puts most of my work upstream: framing problems where the information is incomplete, building consensus with people who don't work in digital, explaining design decisions to technical and non-technical audiences, and setting standards that hold after I've moved on.

I'm a trained GDS service assessor and have sat on assessment panels several times. I'm Brazilian, based in Manchester, and I work in Portuguese and English.
